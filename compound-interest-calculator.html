How compound interest is calculated here

This calculator uses the standard compound interest formula A = P(1 + r/n)^(nt) for your starting amount, plus the future-value-of-an-annuity formula for any regular monthly contributions you add on top. More frequent compounding (daily vs. annually) produces a slightly higher return at the same stated interest rate — that's why the compounding frequency option matters.

Compound interest is often called one of the most powerful forces in personal finance, and the reason is straightforward once you see it in numbers: interest earns interest. A simple interest account only ever pays a return on your original deposit. A compound interest account pays a return on your original deposit *plus* every dollar of interest it has already earned, which means the growth curve isn't a straight line — it accelerates over time. The difference is barely noticeable over one or two years, but becomes dramatic over ten, twenty, or thirty years, which is why starting early matters more than the size of any individual contribution.

Compounding frequency changes the outcome even at the same stated annual rate, because it determines how often that "interest earns interest" effect actually kicks in. Daily compounding recalculates and adds interest 365 times a year; annual compounding does it once. At the same 5% annual rate, daily compounding will produce a slightly higher actual return than annual compounding, because each day's tiny bit of interest starts earning its own interest immediately, rather than waiting a full year. The difference is usually small in percentage terms but can add up meaningfully over long periods or large balances.

Adding a regular monthly contribution changes the shape of the growth curve substantially. Over long time horizons — 15, 20, 30 years — consistent contributions often end up contributing more to the final balance than the original starting amount did, simply because each new contribution gets decades to compound rather than just the initial deposit getting all that time. This is the mathematical basis behind "pay yourself first" savings advice: the earlier and more consistently money is added, the more time it has to benefit from compounding.

Frequently asked questions

Does adding monthly contributions actually make a big difference?

Often more than the starting amount does over long time horizons — consistent contributions compound alongside your principal. Try comparing the result with and without a contribution to see the gap.

What compounding frequency should I choose?

Match what your actual account uses — most savings accounts and investment accounts compound daily or monthly. Annual compounding is mostly for simple illustrative comparisons.

Is this the same as an investment return calculator?

Conceptually yes — this models any growth process where interest is calculated on an increasing balance, which covers both savings accounts and investment growth.
