How this savings goal calculator works

This is the reverse of a standard compound interest calculator. Instead of starting with a monthly contribution and calculating where you'll end up, this tool starts with where you want to end up and solves for the monthly contribution that gets you there — accounting for the fact that both your existing savings and every future contribution keep earning interest along the way.

That last part matters more than people expect. A contribution made in year one earns interest for the entire remaining period; a contribution made in the final month barely earns any interest at all. The math here correctly weights every contribution by how long it actually has to grow, rather than treating them all the same.

Why the same goal can need very different monthly amounts

Three things determine your required monthly contribution, and small changes to any one of them shift the number more than intuition suggests. A longer time horizon doesn't just spread the same total across more months — it also lets each contribution compound for longer, so doubling your timeline can cut the required monthly amount by more than half. A higher interest rate has a similar compounding effect: the difference between a 2% and a 5% savings account isn't a small edge, it's the difference between needing meaningfully more or less of your own money to hit the same number.

Your starting balance matters too, and not in a simple linear way. Money you've already saved has the maximum possible time to compound before your deadline, so an extra $1,000 in your starting balance today is worth more toward your goal than an extra $1,000 contributed near the end — which is part of why the calculator can sometimes tell you no further contribution is needed at all, if your existing savings are already on track to grow into the goal on their own.

A practical way to use this tool

Try running the same goal with a few different time horizons — say, 3 years, 5 years, and 10 years — to see how dramatically the required monthly amount changes. This is often more useful than fixating on a single answer, since it shows you directly what an extra year or two of patience is actually worth in dollars saved per month, which can make a stretch goal feel achievable once you see the more realistic monthly number a longer timeline produces.

Frequently asked questions

Why does it say I don't need to save anything?

This happens when the interest your current savings will earn on its own, by the time your deadline arrives, already meets or exceeds your goal — no additional monthly contribution is needed.

Does this account for interest on my monthly contributions too?

Yes — each monthly contribution starts earning its own interest from the month it's added, not just your starting balance. This is the same compounding math used in our compound interest calculator.

What interest rate should I use?

Use the actual rate on the account you're saving in — a high-yield savings account, a CD, or an investment account's expected return. A more conservative (lower) rate gives a more cautious, dependable monthly target.

Why does a longer time horizon change the monthly amount so much?

A longer timeline does two things at once: it spreads your goal across more contributions, and it gives each contribution more time to compound. Both effects push the required monthly amount down, which is why patience is often the cheapest way to reach a savings goal.
