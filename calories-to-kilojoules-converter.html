The exact figure

1 kcal = 4.184 kJ exactly — and 1 kJ = 0.239006 kcal

The thermochemical calorie is defined as exactly 4.184 joules, so the conversion carries no uncertainty. A rough mental version is to multiply calories by four and add a bit, or divide kilojoules by four and subtract a bit.

The joule is the SI unit of energy and applies to everything — food, electricity, mechanical work — which is why countries that fully adopted metric measures use kilojoules on food labels rather than maintaining a separate food-specific unit.

The capital C problem

The word "calorie" means two different things differing by a factor of a thousand. A scientific calorie is the energy needed to raise one gram of water by one degree Celsius. A nutritional Calorie — conventionally capitalised — is a kilocalorie, a thousand of those.

Every food label uses the second. A chocolate bar listed at 250 calories contains 250 kcal, which is 250,000 scientific calories, or 1,046 kJ. The capitalisation convention that was supposed to distinguish them is almost universally ignored, so context has to do the work: if the number relates to food, it is kilocalories.

This is why the converter above is labelled kcal rather than cal. Treating a food label's number as scientific calories understates the energy by a factor of a thousand.

Which countries use which

Australia and New Zealand label food in kilojoules, with calories often absent entirely. The European Union requires both, printed side by side. The United States, Canada and the United Kingdom lead with calories, though UK labels frequently show kilojoules as well.

The practical consequence for travellers is that an Australian menu showing 8,700 kJ as a daily reference intake is stating the same thing as a British menu showing 2,000 kcal. Neither number is more scientific than the other; they are the same quantity in different units.

What the numbers do and do not tell you

Energy content is measured by how much heat a food releases when burned, adjusted for what the body can actually absorb. That is a real physical quantity and the conversion above is exact arithmetic on it.

What it does not capture is how the body responds. Digestion itself consumes energy, and it consumes noticeably more for protein than for fat or carbohydrate. Fibre passes through largely unabsorbed. Food processing and preparation change availability. Two foods with identical labelled energy can behave differently, which is why energy content alone is an incomplete description of a diet.

This tool converts units and nothing more. Anything about intake, deficits or nutritional adequacy is a question for a doctor or registered dietitian who can account for your circumstances — the arithmetic here cannot.

What people get wrong

Confusing the two calories. A food label’s "calorie" is a kilocalorie — a thousand scientific calories. Treating a 250-calorie snack as 250 scientific calories understates its energy a thousandfold.

Assuming kilojoules mean something different. An Australian label showing 8,700 kJ and a British one showing 2,000 kcal state the same quantity. Neither unit is more scientific than the other.

Frequently asked questions

How many kilojoules in a calorie?

One nutritional calorie, which is a kilocalorie, equals exactly 4.184 kJ. A 250-calorie snack contains 1,046 kJ.

Why does a calorie sometimes mean a kilocalorie?

Because two units share the name. A scientific calorie is a thousandth of a nutritional Calorie. All food labels use the larger one, so any number on packaging is kilocalories.

Which countries use kilojoules on food labels?

Australia and New Zealand use kilojoules, the EU requires both, and the US, Canada and UK lead with calories. An Australian 8,700 kJ daily reference is the same as a British 2,000 kcal.
