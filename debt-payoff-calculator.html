How this debt payoff calculator works

This is the mathematical inverse of a standard loan calculator. A loan calculator starts with a term — say, 5 years — and solves for the monthly payment that pays it off exactly on schedule. This tool works backward: you tell it the payment you're actually making, and it solves for how many months that payment will take to clear the balance, using the same underlying amortization math.

One real constraint worth understanding: if your payment is lower than the interest accruing each month, the balance mathematically can never go down — you'd be paying forever without making progress. This calculator catches that case directly and tells you the minimum payment needed just to stop the balance from growing, rather than showing a nonsensical result.

Why the minimum payment box above matters

Card issuers typically set minimum payments low on purpose — often around 2% of your balance or a small flat floor like $25, whichever is larger. That's usually just enough to cover most of the month's interest, meaning very little of a minimum payment actually reduces what you owe. The comparison box above shows this directly: the same balance, paid at the typical minimum, routinely takes years longer and costs multiples more in total interest than a meaningfully larger fixed payment.

Official resource: the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau's credit card guide covers your rights as a borrower, how to dispute charges, and options if you're struggling with payments.

Snowball or avalanche: what the arithmetic actually says

When you owe money on several things at once, the payment budget is shared and the only real decision is which debt gets the spare money after every minimum is covered. Two methods dominate the advice. The avalanche targets the highest interest rate first. The snowball targets the smallest balance first, on the reasoning that clearing something entirely is motivating.

They only diverge when the smallest debt is not also the most expensive one — and when it does, here is what the difference costs. Take $15,000 spread across three debts with a $650 monthly budget: a $1,500 car loan at 6%, a $4,500 store card at 14%, and a $9,000 credit card at 24.99%.

$15,000 across three debts, $650 per month
MethodTime to clearTotal interestFirst debt gone
Avalanche (highest rate first)29 months$3,797.78month 25
Snowball (smallest balance first)31 months$4,816.03month 5

The avalanche wins on money: $1,018.24 less interest and two months sooner. That is the answer arithmetic gives, and it is the answer most calculators stop at.

But look at the last column, because it is the honest part of this comparison. Under the snowball you clear a debt entirely at month 5. Under the avalanche you clear nothing until month 25 — more than two years of paying without a single account closing. If that difference is what determines whether you stick with the plan at all, the snowball's $1,018 is not a loss, it is what you paid for a strategy you actually finished. A plan abandoned in month nine costs far more than a thousand dollars.

The practical read: run both, look at the gap, and decide honestly which version of yourself is more likely to still be paying in two years. If the gap is small, take the snowball and the early win. If it is large — and it grows with the rate spread between your debts — the avalanche is worth the patience.

What order does not change

Whichever method you pick, three things stay true. Every debt still needs its minimum every month, or you invite late fees and credit damage that dwarf any ordering advantage. The budget should stay fixed as debts clear — the entire power of both methods comes from rolling a cleared debt's payment into the next one rather than absorbing it back into spending. And a rate above roughly 20% is usually worth attacking before contributing to anything other than an employer retirement match, because very little else reliably returns 20%.

Two things sit outside this calculation entirely and are worth checking before you commit to any ordering. A balance transfer at 0% can beat any repayment sequence if you clear the balance inside the promotional window, though the transfer fee and the post-promotional rate decide whether it really does — the debt consolidation walkthrough works through that arithmetic with real numbers. And if the total is genuinely unmanageable rather than merely uncomfortable, a non-profit credit counselling agency can negotiate rates in ways no calculator can model.

Frequently asked questions

Why does it say my payment can never pay off the balance?

This happens when your monthly payment is less than the interest accruing each month — the balance would actually grow, not shrink, no matter how long you paid. You need a payment higher than the monthly interest amount shown in the error for the balance to ever go down.

What is the minimum payment trap?

Credit card minimum payments are typically calculated as a small percentage of your balance (often 2%) or a flat floor like $25, whichever is greater. Paying only the minimum can stretch payoff to a decade or more and multiply the total interest paid many times over compared to a larger fixed payment.

Is this the same math as a loan calculator?

It's the mathematical inverse. A loan calculator starts with a term and solves for the payment; this tool starts with a payment and solves for how long it takes, using the same underlying amortization formula.

Is the debt snowball or the debt avalanche better?

The avalanche always costs less in interest, because it attacks the most expensive debt first. In a $15,000 example across three debts it saved $1,018.24 and two months. The snowball clears its first debt at month 5 against the avalanche's month 25, so it is the better choice if the early win is what keeps you paying.

Should I pay off debt or save for retirement first?

A debt above roughly 20% is usually worth clearing before anything except an employer retirement match, since very little else reliably returns 20%. An employer match is an immediate guaranteed return and is generally worth taking even while carrying debt.
