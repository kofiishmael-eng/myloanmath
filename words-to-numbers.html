How to convert words to numbers

Type a number the way you'd write it in words — "twelve thousand", "negative fifty", "one lakh" — and this words to numbers converter parses it straight back into digits.

This is the reverse problem of writing a number out in words, and it comes up more often than people expect. If you've ever received a document, contract, or old handwritten cheque where the amount is spelled out but you need the actual digit value — to enter into a spreadsheet, double-check a total, or verify that the written amount matches the numeric one — you need exactly this kind of conversion. It's also useful for anyone transcribing dictated numbers, checking legal document accuracy, or working with data that was originally captured as text rather than digits.

The parser understands "point" as the marker for decimals (so "twelve point five" becomes 12.5), and "negative" or "minus" as a prefix for negative numbers. It also recognizes both the international number words (thousand, million, billion) and the Indian system words (lakh, crore) in the same box — you don't need to tell it which system you're using, it figures that out from the words themselves. Hyphenated numbers like "thirty-four" and their spaced equivalent "thirty four" are both understood identically, so formatting quirks in how the text was originally written won't trip it up.

If you type something the tool doesn't recognize — a typo, an unsupported word, or a non-numeric term — you'll get a specific error naming the exact word it couldn't parse, rather than a wrong answer or a generic failure. That's a deliberate design choice: a silent wrong answer is worse than no answer at all when you're working with numbers that matter.

The ambiguities a parser has to resolve

Converting words back into digits is harder than going the other way, because English is not consistent about how spoken numbers map to values. A few cases come up constantly.

The stray "and". British writing produces one hundred and five for 105, but "and" is also how people join separate quantities. The parser treats it as a connector inside a single number rather than as an addition operator, so both one hundred five and one hundred and five return 105.

Years and dates. Nineteen eighty-four is a year, not 19 followed by 84, and it means 1984. Spoken dates follow a different grammar from spoken quantities, and a general-purpose number parser will not always read them the way a person does. If you are transcribing dates, check the result rather than assuming.

Missing scale words. Twenty-five hundred means 2,500 in ordinary American speech, even though the formal written form is two thousand five hundred. Amounts dictated over the phone are full of this kind of shorthand.

Repeated scales. Two million three hundred thousand is 2,300,000, and the second scale word applies only to the group in front of it. This is the case most hand-written conversions get wrong, usually by dropping a factor of ten somewhere in the middle.

When the written amount and the digits disagree

The most common reason to convert words back into digits is verification: you have a document where an amount appears in both forms and you want to confirm they match. It is worth knowing what happens when they do not.

Under the Uniform Commercial Code, which governs negotiable instruments across the United States, the written words control when they conflict with the figures on a cheque or similar instrument. So a mismatch is not merely a typo to be reconciled in whichever direction seems more likely — it changes the legally operative amount to whatever the words say. If you are checking a batch of documents and find a discrepancy, the words are the number that matters, and the figure is the one that needs correcting.

Contracts follow a similar principle in most jurisdictions for the same practical reason: a spelled-out amount is far harder to alter after signing than a row of digits.

Transcribing dictated or handwritten amounts

Two habits prevent most errors. First, convert and compare rather than reading and typing — running the written form through a parser and checking it against the figure catches transposition mistakes that the eye slides straight past, because 4,270 and 4,720 look far more alike than "four thousand two hundred seventy" and "four thousand seven hundred twenty" sound. Second, treat any unrecognised word as a stop signal rather than something to guess at. This converter names the exact word it could not parse instead of silently returning a plausible wrong number, which is the behaviour you want when the output is going into a ledger.

Frequently asked questions

Can I type numbers with hyphens like "thirty-four"?

Yes, both "thirty-four" and "thirty four" are recognized.

Does it understand "lakh" and "crore"?

Yes — Indian number words like lakh and crore are recognized alongside the standard thousand/million/billion words.

What happens if I type a word it doesn't recognize?

You'll get a clear error message naming the exact word it didn't understand, rather than a wrong silent answer.

Does it understand twenty-five hundred?

Yes. Twenty-five hundred is ordinary American shorthand for 2,500, and it parses the same as two thousand five hundred.

Will it read nineteen eighty-four as a year?
