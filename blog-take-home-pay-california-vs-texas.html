Take-Home Pay: California vs. Texas

Published 2026-07-31 · Updated 2026-08-02

"How much more would I actually take home in Texas?" is one of the most common questions behind a relocation or remote-work decision, and it has a precise answer — because federal tax and FICA are identical in every state, the entire gap between California and Texas take-home pay comes down to exactly one number: California’s state income tax. Here is what that looks like at four salary levels, computed exactly the way our salary calculator does it.

The pattern worth noticing

The gap does not stay flat as income rises — it grows, because California’s tax is progressive while Texas’s is a flat zero. For higher earners specifically, the state-tax gap between a state like California and a no-income-tax state widens considerably faster than income does. At $75,000 the difference is about $143.88 a month; at $250,000 it is $1,471.72 a month.

What this comparison doesn’t include

State income tax is one line in a much larger budget, and for this pair the counterweight is property tax. Texas funds itself without an income tax partly through property tax rates that rank among the highest in the country, and it has no equivalent of California's Proposition 13 limit on how fast an assessment can rise. A Texas homeowner can therefore hand back a large share of the income-tax saving through the annual property tax bill, while a Texas renter keeps far more of it.

A note on precision: these figures apply each state’s bracket schedule to your federal taxable income. Texas levies no personal income tax on wages, so its column is federal tax and FICA only. California’s figure comes from its own published brackets. States also have their own credits, exemptions and deductions that this model does not carry, so treat the numbers as a close estimate of the gap rather than a filled-in tax return. The methodology page sets out exactly what is and is not modelled.
