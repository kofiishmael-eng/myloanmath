Why stacked discounts don't just add up

"20% off, plus an extra 10% off" feels like it should mean 30% off total — but it doesn't, and the gap is bigger than people expect on larger purchases. The second discount applies to the price after the first discount already reduced it, not to the original price. On a $100 item: 20% off brings it to $80, then 10% off that $80 brings it to $72 — a combined discount of 28%, not 30%. The two percentages compound against a shrinking base rather than simply adding together.

This matters most when a store advertises "extra 20% off already-reduced items" — the true final price is always a bit higher than naively subtracting both percentages from the original price would suggest. The gap between the naive assumption and the real number grows with the size of the discounts: two 50% discounts stacked feels like "free," but the real math is 1 - (0.5 × 0.5) = 75% off, not 100%.

The math behind sale prices, in plain terms

A discount percentage converts to a multiplier by subtracting it from 100%, then dividing by 100. A 25% discount means you pay 75% of the original price, so the sale price is the original price times 0.75. This is the same operation whether you're calculating a single discount or working out one leg of a stacked discount — each discount in sequence multiplies the running price by (1 − that discount as a decimal).

Working backward — figuring out what discount percentage a sale price represents — uses the same relationship in reverse: divide the amount saved by the original price, then convert to a percentage. If something cost $80 and is now $60, you saved $20, and $20 divided by $80 is 0.25, or 25% off. This reverse calculation is exactly what the second calculator on this page does automatically.

Where rounding causes small discrepancies

Real store receipts sometimes show a sale price that's a cent or two different from what a pure percentage calculation produces, because retailers often round the price to the nearest cent at each step of a multi-discount calculation rather than carrying the exact fractional value through to the end. On small purchases this difference is invisible; on larger ones, like furniture or electronics, it can occasionally add up to a few cents of difference between this calculator's result and a receipt's exact total. That's normal and not a sign either number is wrong — they're just rounding at different points.

Frequently asked questions

Do two discounts add together?

No — this is a common mistake. 20% off, then an additional 10% off, is not 30% off total. The second discount applies to the already-discounted price, so the real combined discount is 28%, not 30%.

How do I find the discount percentage if I only know the original and sale price?

Use the reverse calculator below the main tool — enter both prices, and it calculates the exact discount percentage and amount saved for you.

Why does a store receipt sometimes show a slightly different sale price?

Retailers often round to the nearest cent at each step of a multi-discount calculation, rather than carrying the exact decimal value through. This can create a tiny difference of a cent or two on larger purchases — it doesn't mean either calculation is wrong.

Can a discount ever exceed 100%?

No — a 100% discount means the item is free. Anything entered above 100% isn't a meaningful real-world discount, since you can't pay a negative price for something in a standard sale.
