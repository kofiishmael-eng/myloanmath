Four ways to calculate a percentage

Percentage questions come in different shapes depending on what you already know. This calculator covers the four you'll run into most often: finding a percentage of a number, finding what percentage one number is of another, finding the percentage change between two values, and applying a percentage increase or decrease to a starting number.

These four modes cover the situations that actually come up in everyday life and work, even though they look similar at a glance. "X% of Y" answers questions like calculating a tip, a discount, or a commission — you know the percentage and the base amount, and want the resulting figure. "X is what % of Y" flips that around — useful for figuring out what portion a specific number represents of a total, like what percentage of a budget was spent on one category, or what percentage of a class scored above a certain grade.

Percentage change is a different calculation entirely, and it's the one most commonly done wrong by hand. Going from 50 to 75 is a 50% increase, but going from 75 back down to 50 is not a 50% decrease — it's roughly a 33% decrease, because the base you're measuring against changed. This asymmetry trips people up constantly in contexts like stock prices, sales figures, and salary comparisons, where the direction of the comparison matters. This tool also correctly handles percentage change when the starting value is negative, which is a genuine edge case that many simple percentage calculators get backwards.

The fourth mode, applying an increase or decrease, is the reverse of percentage change — instead of comparing two known values, you start with one value and a percentage, and calculate the result. A positive percentage increases the value, a negative percentage decreases it, which lets you model things like price increases, discounts, or projected growth without needing two separate tools.

Percent and percentage point are not the same thing

This is the single most consequential confusion in the subject, and it appears constantly in news reporting about interest rates and taxes. If a rate rises from 4% to 6%, that is an increase of two percentage points, but an increase of 50 percent. Both descriptions are correct and they differ by a factor of twenty-five.

The practical rule: percentage points describe the arithmetic difference between two percentages, while percent describes the proportional change between them. Whenever a figure is quoted as a change in something that was already a percentage — a rate, a share, a margin — it is worth establishing which of the two is meant before drawing a conclusion, because the more dramatic-sounding version is frequently the one chosen.

Increases and decreases are not symmetric

A 50% fall followed by a 50% rise does not return you to where you started. Something worth $100 that drops 50% is worth $50; a subsequent 50% rise brings it to $75, not $100. Recovering from a 50% loss requires a 100% gain, because the second percentage is applied to a smaller base.

The asymmetry grows sharply as the decline deepens: recovering from a 20% fall needs a 25% gain, from a 50% fall needs 100%, and from an 80% fall needs 400%. This is why proportional losses matter more than proportional gains of the same size, and it is the arithmetic behind a great deal of investment advice that otherwise sounds like superstition.

Working backwards from a final amount

A frequent real-world need is the reverse calculation: you know the amount after a percentage was applied and want the amount before. The instinct is to subtract the same percentage, and it is wrong. If an item costs $80 after a 20% discount, the original price is not $96 — it is $80 divided by 0.8, which is $100.

The same applies to tax-inclusive prices. To strip 20% tax from a $120 total you divide by 1.2 to get $100, rather than taking 20% of $120. Subtracting instead of dividing is one of the most common arithmetic errors in everyday finance, and the size of the error grows with the percentage involved.

Stacked percentages don't add up

Two successive discounts of 20% and 10% do not make 30%. The second is applied to the already-reduced price, so $100 becomes $80, then $72 — an effective discount of 28%. The order does not matter, but the total is always less than the sum of the parts.

The same effect works in reverse for increases: two consecutive 10% rises produce 21%, not 20%, because the second compounds on the first. Any time percentages are applied one after another rather than simultaneously, they compound rather than add.

Frequently asked questions

What is 15% of 200?

Use the "X% of Y" mode with X=15 and Y=200 — the answer is 30.

How do I calculate percentage change between two numbers?

Use the "% change from X to Y" mode. It correctly handles negative starting values too, which trips up a lot of simple percentage calculators.

How do I calculate a percentage increase?

Use "Increase / decrease X by Y%" with a positive Y for an increase, or a negative Y for a decrease.

What is the difference between percent and percentage points?

Percentage points describe the arithmetic gap between two percentages; percent describes the proportional change between them. A rise from 4% to 6% is two percentage points, and also a 50 percent increase.

How do I find the original price before a discount?

Divide rather than subtract. An item costing $80 after 20% off had an original price of $80 divided by 0.8, which is $100 \u2014 not $96.

Do a 20% discount and a 10% discount add up to 30%?

No. The second applies to the already-reduced price, so $100 becomes $80 and then $72, an effective 28%. Percentages applied one after another compound rather than add.
