Buying a $350,000 House: A Complete Walkthrough

By Ishmael, accountant · Published 2026-07-28

Most mortgage advice talks in generalities. This is a specific, worked example instead — a real $350,000 home, a real down payment, and the exact monthly number that results, calculated the same way our mortgage calculator and savings goal calculator compute it.

The setup

A $350,000 home, 10% down ($35,000), a 6.5% interest rate on a 30-year mortgage. 10% down is a realistic, common scenario — enough to be a meaningful down payment, but below the 20% threshold that avoids mortgage insurance, which is exactly the kind of detail worth seeing play out in real numbers rather than skipped over.

The monthly payment, piece by piece
Loan amount financed ($350,000 − $35,000)$315,000.00
Principal & interest$1,991.01
Property tax (est. 1.1% annually)$320.83
Home insurance$125.00
PMI (since down payment is under 20%)$131.25
Total monthly payment$2,568.09

Why PMI shows up here, and what it actually costs

Because the down payment is 10%, not 20%, the lender requires private mortgage insurance — protection for the lender, not the buyer, in case of default. Here it adds $131.25 a month, or about $1,575 a year. This isn't a permanent cost: once enough of the loan is paid down to reach 20% equity, PMI can typically be removed, which is one real reason to track your loan balance over time rather than just set the payment and forget it.

What the "principal and interest" line actually hides

That $1,991.01 is a fixed number for the full 30 years, but what it's made of changes completely over time. In the first year, the overwhelming majority of each payment goes to interest, not principal — this is normal amortization behavior, not a red flag, and it's exactly why paying extra toward principal early in a mortgage saves more in total interest than the same extra payment made later. Over the full 30-year term, the total interest paid on this loan comes to $401,765.14 — more than the home's original price, which is the real, unglamorous math of what a 30-year mortgage costs when interest is included.

Saving the $35,000 down payment

Say $5,000 is already saved, with a goal of reaching the full $35,000 in two years, in a savings account earning 4% annually. Working backward through the same math our savings goal calculator uses:

That last line is worth sitting with: even over just two years, the interest earned on the growing balance covers more than a full month's worth of contributions on its own. The number that actually matters for planning is the $1,186.08 monthly contribution — that's the real, concrete target, not just "save more."

Closing costs, the number that catches first-time buyers

The down payment is the figure everyone saves toward, and it is not the only cash needed at completion. Closing costs typically run around two to five percent of the purchase price and are paid separately — on a $350,000 house that is roughly $7,000 to $17,500 on top of the deposit.

They cover lender origination charges, appraisal, title search and title insurance, recording fees, attorney fees where applicable, and prepaid items such as the first year of homeowners insurance and an initial escrow deposit. A buyer who saved exactly the down payment and nothing more is short at the worst possible moment, and this is the single most common cash-flow surprise in a first purchase.

The costs that start the day you move in

Ownership adds recurring costs that renting does not. Maintenance is the one most often left out entirely: a common planning figure is around one percent of the home's value each year, averaged over time. It arrives unevenly — nothing for three years, then a roof — which is exactly why it needs a sinking fund rather than a hope.

Add utilities that a landlord previously covered, and the immediate one-off costs of moving in: appliances, window coverings, tools, and the repairs that only become visible once you live there. Our rent vs. buy calculator includes maintenance as an explicit input rather than burying it, because leaving it out is what makes buying look cheaper than it is.

Getting the PMI removed

Private mortgage insurance protects the lender, not you, and it is not permanent. Under US federal rules it must automatically terminate once the loan balance reaches 78% of the original purchase price on schedule, and you can generally request cancellation at 80%.

Two things make it worth acting rather than waiting. Requesting cancellation at 80% is earlier than automatic termination at 78%, and the difference is real money each month. And if the home's value has risen, an appraisal may show you have reached the threshold well ahead of the schedule — the balance is what the schedule tracks, but appreciation counts toward equity too. The request usually needs to be in writing with a current payment record.

Paying extra toward principal reaches the threshold sooner, and our extra payment calculator shows how much sooner for any given amount.

Frequently asked questions

How much cash do I need beyond the down payment?

Closing costs typically run two to five percent of the purchase price — roughly $7,000 to $17,500 on a $350,000 house — covering lender fees, appraisal, title work, and prepaid insurance and escrow.

How much should I budget for home maintenance?

A common planning figure is about one percent of the home’s value each year, averaged over time. It arrives unevenly, so it needs a dedicated fund rather than being absorbed month to month.

When can I stop paying PMI?

Under US federal rules it terminates automatically at 78% of the original purchase price on schedule, and you can generally request cancellation at 80%. Rising home values or extra principal payments can reach the threshold sooner.
