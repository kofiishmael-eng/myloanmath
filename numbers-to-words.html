How to convert a number to words

Type a number into the box above — whole numbers, decimals, and negative numbers all work. This number to words converter reads it back the way you'd say it out loud or write it on a form: "one thousand two hundred thirty-four" instead of "1234".

Writing numbers out in words matters more than it might seem. Legal documents, cheques, contracts, and formal invoices often require an amount to appear both as digits and spelled out — this isn't a formality, it's a fraud-prevention measure. Digits can be altered with a single stroke of a pen (a 1 becomes a 7, a 0 becomes an 8), but a word like "one thousand" is far harder to quietly edit into "seven thousand" without it being obvious. That's why banks, courts, and accountants have required both formats for centuries, long before typed documents existed.

This tool supports two different numbering systems because the world doesn't group large numbers the same way everywhere. The international system — the one used across North America, the UK, and most of Europe — groups digits in sets of three: thousand, million, billion, trillion. The Indian numbering system groups differently after the first three digits, using lakh (100,000) and crore (10,000,000) instead. So the number 10,000,000 is "Ten Million" in the international system but "One Crore" in the Indian system — same number, completely different word structure. If you're filling out a form for an Indian bank, government office, or legal document, using the wrong system can cause real confusion, so this tool lets you switch between them with one click rather than making you do the conversion in your head.

Decimals are read digit-by-digit after the word "point" — this matches how numbers are actually spoken aloud, rather than trying to spell out fractions, which gets confusing fast (nobody says "twelve and five-tenths" in casual speech). Negative numbers simply get "Negative" prefixed to the front, exactly as you'd expect.

The rules people actually get wrong

Hyphens. Every compound number from twenty-one to ninety-nine takes a hyphen: forty-two, seventy-six, ninety-nine. Nothing else does. It is one hundred forty-two, not one-hundred forty-two, because the hundred is not a compound. This is the single most common mistake on formal documents.

The word "and". American usage omits it: one hundred five. British usage includes it before the final part: one hundred and five. Neither is wrong, but mixing them inside one document looks careless, and on a cheque the "and" is conventionally reserved for separating the whole amount from the cents. This converter follows American convention, so nothing is inserted where a British writer would expect it.

Commas. Written-out numbers do not take commas the way digits do. 1,234 becomes one thousand two hundred thirty-four, with no comma anywhere. Commas appear only when you are listing separate numbers.

Zero in the middle. 1,005 is one thousand five, not one thousand zero hundred five. Empty groups are simply skipped, which is why 1,000,005 reads as one million five.

Writing an amount on a cheque

The words line on a cheque is the legal amount, and it is worth knowing the conventions. Write the whole-dollar amount in words, then the cents as a fraction over 100 — Four hundred twenty-seven and 63/100. If the amount is a round figure, the cents are still written: and 00/100, or and no/100. Start hard against the left edge and draw a horizontal line through whatever space is left over, so nothing can be added after you sign.

This matters because of what happens in a dispute. Under the Uniform Commercial Code, which governs negotiable instruments across the United States, when the written words and the numeric figures on an instrument contradict each other, the words govern. A cheque with "$4,270.00" in the box and "Four hundred twenty-seven and 63/100" on the line is a cheque for $427.63. That rule is exactly why both forms are required in the first place: digits can be altered with a single pen stroke, words cannot be edited quietly, so the harder-to-forge version is the one that counts.

Short scale, long scale, and the Indian system

A billion is not the same number everywhere. Under the short scale used in the United States and in modern British usage, a billion is a thousand million (109). Under the long scale historically used in Britain and still used across much of continental Europe, a billion is a million million (1012) and the intermediate value is a milliard. Britain officially moved to the short scale for government statistics in 1974, but documents predating that, and translations from European sources, can still mean the older value. If a number came from an unfamiliar source and the magnitude looks implausible, this is often why.

The Indian numbering system groups differently again. Rather than grouping in threes, it groups the first three digits then in twos: 1,00,000 is one lakh and 1,00,00,000 is one crore. Switching on Indian numbering above changes the grouping rather than the number, so 10,000,000 reads as "One Crore" instead of "Ten Million".

Frequently asked questions

What is a number to words converter used for?

It's used any time you need a number written out in full — legal documents, cheques, contracts, invoices, and forms that require the amount spelled out rather than shown as digits.

Does it support the Indian numbering system?

Yes — turn on "Indian numbering" to get lakh and crore groupings (e.g. 10,00,000 as "Ten Lakh") instead of the international thousand/million/billion groupings.

Can it convert decimals and negative numbers?

Yes. Decimals are read digit-by-digit after "point" (e.g. 12.5 becomes "Twelve Point Five"), and negative numbers are prefixed with "Negative".

Do I hyphenate one hundred forty-two?

Only the compound part. Numbers from twenty-one to ninety-nine take a hyphen, so it is one hundred forty-two — the hyphen belongs in forty-two, not after hundred.

Should I write one hundred and five, or one hundred five?

Both are correct, in different places. American usage omits the and; British usage includes it. On a cheque the and is conventionally kept for separating dollars from cents, as in four hundred twenty-seven and 63/100.

If the words and figures on a cheque disagree, which one counts?

The words. Under the Uniform Commercial Code, when the written amount and the numeric amount on a negotiable instrument conflict, the written words govern. That is the whole reason both are required.
