Currency to words, done correctly per currency

Not every currency splits into 100 cents — Japanese Yen has no minor unit at all, and Kuwaiti and Bahraini Dinars split into 1,000 (not 100). This currency to words converter handles each of the 65+ supported currencies with its correct subunit name and decimal precision, not a one-size-fits-all "dollars and cents" template.

This distinction matters more than it might seem for anyone working across borders — international invoices, cross-border contracts, or documentation for a multinational business. Getting a currency's subunit wrong isn't just a cosmetic mistake; it can make a formal document look unprofessional or, worse, genuinely ambiguous about the amount involved. A British Pound splits into Pence, not Cents. A Saudi Riyal splits into Halalas. A Russian Ruble splits into Kopecks. Each of these has its own correct name, and this tool uses the right one automatically based on which currency you select, rather than defaulting to "cents" for everything.

The decimal precision differs too, and this is where most simple currency converters get it wrong. Most currencies use two decimal places, matching the familiar dollars-and-cents pattern. But Kuwaiti Dinars, Bahraini Dinars, Iraqi Dinars, Jordanian Dinars, Omani Rials, and Tunisian Dinars all use three decimal places — meaning their smallest unit is a thousandth of the main currency, not a hundredth. And currencies like the Japanese Yen, Korean Won, Vietnamese Dong, and Chilean Peso have no minor unit in everyday use at all, so amounts round to whole units with no fractional cents shown.

This tool spells out the subunit in full — "and Fifty Cents," "and Twenty Pence" — which is the right format for contracts, legal writing, and formal correspondence where the full written-out amount needs to be unambiguous. That's different from the banking-standard fraction format ("50/100") used specifically on physical cheques, since cheques and formal documents follow different writing conventions on purpose.

How the amount line on a cheque should be written

Write the whole units in words, then the fractional part as a number over one hundred: Four hundred twenty-seven and 63/100. For a round amount the fraction is still written, as and 00/100 or and no/100, because a blank space is an invitation to add something. Begin hard against the left edge and draw a horizontal line through whatever space remains after the last word, for the same reason.

Capitalisation is a matter of house style rather than law. Many organisations capitalise the first word only; others capitalise each word, which is what this converter produces so the boundaries between words stay obvious on a handwritten line.

Why both the words and the figures are required

Requiring an amount twice, in two different notations, is a fraud control that predates typed documents. Digits are trivially alterable — a 1 becomes a 7, a 3 becomes an 8, a decimal point moves — whereas altering four hundred into seven hundred without the change being obvious is very difficult.

The rule that follows from this is worth knowing. Under the Uniform Commercial Code, which governs negotiable instruments across the United States, the written words govern when they conflict with the figures. A cheque reading "$4,270.00" in the box and "Four hundred twenty-seven and 63/100" on the line is a cheque for $427.63. Similar principles apply to contracts in most jurisdictions, for the same reason. If you are checking documents and find a mismatch, it is the figure that needs correcting, not the words.

Conventions differ by currency and country

The dollars-and-hundredths pattern is not universal. British cheques conventionally read as pounds with pence expressed either in words or as a fraction, and the word "only" is often appended after the amount to close the line — a convention also widespread across South Asia, where Rupees One Lakh Only is the standard form. Currencies without a fractional subunit, or with subunits divided other than into hundredths, do not use the over-100 fraction at all.

Grouping differs as well. The Indian numbering system groups the first three digits and then in twos, so 1,00,000 is one lakh and 1,00,00,000 is one crore, and an amount written for an Indian bank uses those words rather than hundreds of thousands and tens of millions.

Where else spelled-out amounts are required

Beyond cheques, written amounts appear on promissory notes, loan agreements, settlement documents, invoices in jurisdictions that require it for tax purposes, letters of credit, and most notarised instruments. The requirement is consistently strongest where a document creates an obligation to pay, and weakest where it merely records one.

Frequently asked questions

How many currencies are supported?

65+ world currencies, each with the correct major and minor unit names — Pence for GBP, Fils for AED, Kopecks for RUB, Paise for INR, and so on.

Can I use this for legal documents and contracts?

Yes — this is exactly the format legal and contract writing calls for: the subunit spelled out in full ("and Fifty Cents") rather than shown as a fraction, so the amount reads unambiguously in formal writing.

What does the /100 after the amount mean?

It expresses the cents as a fraction of a whole unit. Sixty-three cents is written as 63/100, and a round amount is written as 00/100 or no/100 so the line cannot be added to.

If the written amount and the figures disagree, which one is legally binding?

For cheques and other negotiable instruments in the United States, the Uniform Commercial Code makes the written words govern. That is the reason both forms are required in the first place.
